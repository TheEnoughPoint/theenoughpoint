**[The last piece](/what-a-mile-is-worth) priced a mile. It left one question open, deliberately — the same question our December 2013 spreadsheet was built to settle: which credit card should you actually carry?**

Here is why we held it back. Answering "which card" with a card is close to useless. Card terms in this market move monthly — one widely held rewards card is being withdrawn in October 2026 — so any list is decaying from the day it publishes. Worse, the list format hides the thing that actually decides your return, which is not the card at all.

It is your spending. Both its size and its shape.

## The rate on the box is a rate on a slice

Every bonus card in Singapore works the same way. It pays a headline rate — typically 4 miles per dollar — on eligible spending up to a monthly cap, and everything past that cap drops to a base rate. That base rate is remarkably consistent across the market: **0.4 miles per dollar**, whether it arrives as one point per dollar at Citi or one unit per five dollars at DBS and UOB. Different arithmetic, same landing place.

So the number on the advertisement describes the first S$1,000 of your month. Your wallet earns the weighted average across all of it.

<CapCliff />

That single chart is most of the article. A card advertised at 4 mpd, held alone by someone spending S$4,000 a month, blends to **1.30 mpd** — *below* a plain 1.4 mpd general-spend card that has no categories to track at all. The card did not underperform. The denominator grew.

And notice what the chart says about the obvious fix. Adding cards genuinely does lift the ceiling: at S$4,000 a month, four caps' worth of capacity holds the blended rate at 4.00 where one card gives 1.30. Three times the miles, same spending, same headline rate.

Which is exactly where most write-ups stop, and where the honest version has to keep going.

## Two ceilings, and only one of them takes a card

The four-card line above assumes something it never states: that your spending arrives pre-sorted into your cards' categories. That S$4,000 of monthly spending distributes neatly across the online cap, the contactless cap, the dining cap and the transport cap, in exactly the proportions those caps allow.

Real spending does nothing of the sort.

A card capped at S$1,000 of **online** spending does nothing for your contactless spending. A second online card does nothing once your online spending is exhausted. **Bonus capacity is category-shaped, not a pool** — and the categories are narrower than they sound. Groceries, insurance premiums, school fees, income tax, rent and mortgage payments are either excluded outright or cannot be charged to a card at all. For most households those are the largest lines in the budget.

The dashed line on the chart is the honest one: four cards, with 60% of spending landing somewhere a bonus applies, earn **2.56 mpd** rather than 4.00. Not because the cards failed, but because the spending was never eligible.

This gives the only diagnostic that matters, and the two answers point in opposite directions:

- **If your caps are the binding constraint** — you have eligible spending overflowing into the base rate — then another card genuinely helps, and the arithmetic will tell you how much.
- **If your categories are the binding constraint** — you have unused capacity sitting on cards you already hold — then **another card changes nothing at all**, and buying one is a fee in exchange for a rounding error.

Most households, on our reading of how the caps and exclusions actually work, are in the second position and believe they are in the first.

Two of those deserve emphasis because they are the ones that quietly reverse a calculation.

**Annual fees are not a rounding error on a stack.** Four cards at roughly S$196 each is about S$780 a year. On S$48,000 of annual spending that is **1.6 percentage points** — larger than the entire advantage of a flat cashback card. Most are waivable on request, and the waiver is at the bank's discretion, which means the assumption "I will just get it waived" is a forecast, not a fact.

**Minimum spends are asymmetric.** Several bonus rates require a monthly floor. Miss it and you do not earn a reduced bonus; you earn the base rate on everything that month. A stack you are not actively operating does not degrade gracefully — it falls off a cliff, quietly, in the months you were busy.

The row worth studying is not the one matching your card count. It is the **next** one down — what one more card would actually add, before you apply for it. On plausible inputs that number is often small enough to be swallowed whole by the annual fee, which is the calculation the application form does not show you.

## At one cent, the comparison is free

The link back to the redemption piece is a coincidence of arithmetic worth memorising.

**One mile per dollar, redeemed at one cent a mile, is a 1% rebate.** So at exactly one cent, miles-per-dollar and percentage rebate are the same number. A blended 1.3 mpd stack is a 1.3% rebate. A blended 2.2 mpd stack is 2.2%.

Which makes the comparison against a cashback card immediate. At the **1.7%** available on the best uncapped flat cashback card, your blended rate simply has to beat **1.7 mpd** — if you cash the miles out at a cent. Below that, the cashback card was the better instrument and required no thought at all.

Above a cent, everything improves, and that is the entire connection between these two articles: **the earning side sets your rate, the redemption side sets your multiplier, and you need both to be right.** A 4 mpd stack redeemed into inflated KrisShop stock is a worse outcome than a 1.7% cashback card. A 1.3 mpd stack redeemed into a business-class seat you would genuinely have paid for is a far better one. The card is half the answer, and it is the less important half.

## So what do you do?

1. **Measure the shape of your spending before looking at any card.** Not the total — the split. How much is online, how much is contactless, how much is rent, tax, insurance and school fees that no card will reward. That distribution determines your ceiling, and no product can change it.

2. **Work out which ceiling binds.** Unused capacity on cards you hold means categories bind, and another card is not the answer. Eligible spending overflowing into the base rate means caps bind, and another card is.

3. **Count the cards you will operate, not the ones you would like to own.** The honest number is the one you will still be routing correctly in the eleventh month, after a busy fortnight and a holiday.

4. **Subtract the running costs before deciding**, and treat fee waivers as requests rather than entitlements.

5. **Then check it against doing nothing.** If your blended rate is under about 1.7 mpd and your miles end up cashed out rather than flown, a single flat cashback card would have paid more, and cost you no attention whatsoever. That is a perfectly respectable answer, and for a great many households it is the right one.

There is a version of this hobby that is genuinely lucrative, and our own [redemption ledger](/what-a-mile-is-worth) is evidence of it. There is also a version that is an elaborate way of earning 1.3% while feeling like you are earning 4. The arithmetic above is the only thing that tells you which one you are running.
