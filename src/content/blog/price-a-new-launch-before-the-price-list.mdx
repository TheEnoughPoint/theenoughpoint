At a new launch preview you meet the price for the first time in a room built to help you decide by the weekend. There is a scale model, a queue, a booking chit, and a number you have had roughly ninety seconds to think about.

Almost everything you would want to know about that number can be worked out weeks earlier, from data that is free and public. Not the price itself — nobody outside the developer knows that until the list is printed. But the land cost, what land has been converting into elsewhere, what the same money buys in floor area two streets away, and how fast the neighbourhood has to rise for you to get out whole. Four of those five are simply arithmetic on published records.

This is the method, run end to end on a real site. **Thomson Reserve** is the redevelopment of the former Thomson View at Bright Hill Drive in District 20, and the developer's own material puts the preview in October 2026. We use it because a method taught on an imaginary project teaches nothing, and because its price list does not exist yet — which is the whole point. Nothing here is a view on the project, and there is no price target in it. When the list lands, run the steps yourself on the numbers that are actually on it.

## Step one — find what the land cost, then ask what that figure includes

Land is the one input a buyer can check before anything else exists. Collective sales and state tenders are both announced publicly, with a price.

The former Thomson View was awarded on **25 November 2024** at **S$810 million** to a consortium of UOL Group, Singapore Land and CapitaLand Development. The site runs to about five hectares at a plot ratio of 2.1 under the 2019 Master Plan. The marketing agent's award announcement puts it at **S$1,178 per square foot per plot ratio**, and — this is the part to read twice — states that the figure is reached *after* factoring in land betterment charges for intensifying the site and the lease upgrading premium for a fresh 99-year lease.

That clause is worth more than the headline. Do the division yourself: S$810 million across roughly 1.13 million square feet of sellable space is nearer **S$714** per square foot per plot ratio. The distance between S$714 and S$1,178 is the two top-ups — payments to the state for building more on the site and for resetting a lease that began in 1975. They are not a footnote. On this site they are close to two-fifths of what the land actually cost.

So the first question at any preview is not "was the land cheap". It is **"is that rate before or after the charges?"** A rate quoted before them understates what the developer paid, and every calculation that follows is then wrong in the developer's favour. If nobody in the room can tell you which it is, the number is not usable yet.

For scale, from URA's own record of awarded residential tenders: awards in 2026 have averaged **S$1,474** per square foot per plot ratio, up **17%** on 2025's S$1,260. The nearest suburban comparisons were Lentor Central at S$1,278 in March and Bayshore Drive at S$1,323 in July. So S$1,178 does sit below land bought in 2026.

One correction to the way this usually gets told, though. This was not land bought long ago and patiently sat on. It was awarded in late 2024, about a year before the tenders it is being compared against. That is buying well in a particular window — a sharper skill than patience, and a thinner cushion than "we bought this years ago" implies.

## Step two — turn land into a price, and notice the rule everyone quotes is out of date

There is a familiar rule of thumb for getting from a land rate to a launch price: construction, financing, marketing and profit roughly double it, so launch price ≈ land × 1.8 to 2.0. It is in agent decks, in comment threads, and — we should say this plainly — in the explainer text of our own free property tool, where it has been sitting unexamined.

We went and checked it, and it is stale.

URA publishes two records that can be joined. One lists every awarded land tender with its price. The other, the monthly developer-sales file, lists every project currently selling with its median new-sale price per square foot and the name of the developer. Where the winning tenderer's registered entity is the same name as the developer URA publishes, the site and the project are the same thing. That match is mechanical — no judgement, no guessing which project sits on which plot — and it produces eight pairs.

| Site awarded | Award | Land, S$ psf ppr | Project now selling | Asking, S$ psf | Multiple | Sold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Orchard Boulevard | Feb 2024 | 1,617 | Upperhouse at Orchard Boulevard | 3,437 | 2.13× | 245 / 301 |
| Pine Grove (Parcel B) | Nov 2023 | 1,223 | Nava Grove | 2,686 | 2.20× | 550 / 552 |
| Bukit Timah Link | Nov 2022 | 1,343 | 8@BT | 3,017 | 2.25× | 111 / 158 |
| Champions Way | Sep 2023 | 904 | Norwood Grand | 2,072 | 2.29× | 315 / 348 |
| Zion Road (Parcel B) | Aug 2024 | 1,304 | Promenade Peak | 3,037 | 2.33× | 425 / 596 |
| River Valley Green (Parcel A) | Jun 2024 | 1,325 | River Green | 3,111 | 2.35× | 492 / 524 |
| De Souza Avenue | Aug 2024 | 841 | The Sen | 2,341 | 2.78× | 133 / 347 |
| Zion Road (Parcel A) | Apr 2024 | 1,202 | Zyon Grand | 3,400 | 2.83× | 634 / 706 |

The multiple runs from **2.13× to 2.83×**, with a median of **2.31×**. Not one of the eight is anywhere near 1.8 to 2.0.

Before that number gets quoted anywhere: **the multiple is not a margin, and it is not a valuation.** It contains however much the whole market moved between the land purchase and today, and here that gap runs 24 to 45 months. URA's price index rose 6.5% over the two years to June 2026 alone. Strip two years of index movement out of every pair — which understates it for the older sites — and the median only falls to about 2.16×, still clear of the old rule. Construction costs have also risen since the rule was coined. Both things push the honest multiple up, and neither tells you a developer is being greedy.

Notice as well that the multiple predicts nothing about whether buyers agree. The two highest in the table are 90% sold and 38% sold. Whatever this ratio measures, it is not demand.

Apply the observed range to a verified S$1,178: land like that has been converting into roughly **S$2,500 to S$3,300** per square foot, with the middle of the pairs near S$2,700. That is a check on a number, not a number. Had we run the old 1.8–2.0 rule, we would have produced about S$2,100 to S$2,400 and concluded that anything above it looked stretched — confidently, and on a rule that no recent pair supports.

For the rest of this article we need prices to work with, and none exist. So we use **S$2,600, S$2,900 and S$3,100 per square foot** — three illustrative figures we chose to straddle what the pairs above imply. They are not a forecast, not anyone's quote and not a price we expect. They are there so you can watch what each step does as the price moves, and swap in the real one in October.

## Step three — the gap over the neighbourhood is not one number

District 20 — Bishan and Ang Mo Kio — is a liquid resale market: **345 condominium resales** in the twelve months to August 2026, at a median of **S$1,962 per square foot**. That is what existing homes there change hands at now.

New homes always cost more per square foot than existing ones, and comparing against a district median is comparing against every vintage, floor and stack in the area at once. The comparison gets sharper when you match on size, because [size drives price per square foot more than the postcode does](/district-yields-price-tells-you-little/) — small flats carry a higher rate almost everywhere.

Here is the same district, split into the size bands our data uses:

| Flat size | District 20 resale median | At S$2,600 | At S$2,900 | At S$3,100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 600 sq ft | S$1,966 | +32% | +48% | +58% |
| 600 – 850 sq ft | S$2,205 | +18% | +32% | +41% |
| 850 – 1,150 sq ft | S$2,042 | +27% | +42% | +52% |
| 1,150 sq ft and over | S$1,763 | +47% | +64% | +76% |

Read the spread, not the rows. One price list, one building, one morning — and the gap over the surrounding market runs from **+18% to +76%** depending only on which door you walk through. Mid-size flats are the tightest-priced against their local comparison; the largest are the most stretched, partly because the large-flat band in this district is weighted towards much older stock, which drags that comparison down and overstates the true gap at the top end.

Two things this table is not. It is **not a measure of overpaying** — part of any new-build gap buys a genuinely newer building, a warranty, no near-term repair bill, and facilities the older stock does not have. And it is not unusual: our own study of 547 new sales across the rest-of-central region put the typical new-launch entry gap at a **median +62.1%** over the contemporaneous district resale median. Measured against that, +32% to +58% is ordinary to low. Anyone telling you a launch is obviously a rip-off, or obviously a steal, has usually skipped this benchmark.

## Step four — hold the budget still and let the size move

A percentage is hard to feel. The same fact in a form you can stand inside: fix the money and let the floor area move.

<SameMoneySize />

That is the entry gap translated out of percentages. The same S$2.4 million is about 828 square feet finished some years from now, or roughly 1,000 to 1,300 square feet you could move into next month — one to two extra bedrooms, in buildings within a few hundred metres of the same stations, with eighty to ninety years of lease still to run.

Two things push back, and both are real.

**The fresh lease is worth money, and the same neighbourhood prices it.** Within 400 metres of a station in this district, Jadescape at 91 years of lease remaining resells at a median S$2,349 per square foot over 62 sales; Braddell View at 54 years resells at S$1,053 over 25 sales. Roughly half. That is an association and not a decay curve — those buildings differ in age, quality, layout and exact location as well as lease, and nothing here separates those effects. But a lease under 60 years does start to restrict how much CPF can be used and how willingly banks lend, and a buyer of a fresh 99-year lease is buying the top of that ladder and a long runway before any of it bites.

**The wait costs money too, and its length is currently unknown.** The developer's own listing gives expected completion as to be announced. Everything about the wait hangs off that one date, so get it in writing before anything is signed rather than after. If you would be renting through it, District 20 rents imply about **S$5,100 a month** on a 1,100 square foot flat — roughly **S$245,000 over four years** at today's rents. If you would be living with family or already own where you live, ignore that paragraph entirely; it only bites if there is rent in the gap. And the instalments during construction are not the developer's choice either — [the payment schedule is set in law, and 60% of the price falls due before the building is finished](/new-launch-or-resale-who-picked-the-dates/).

## Step five — the number that actually decides it

Set aside whether the price is fair. The answerable question is narrower: **what does the neighbourhood have to do for you to get your money back?**

The exit comparable already exists. In about nine years this would be a four-to-five-year-old condominium in District 20 with roughly ninety years of lease left. Five buildings in the district fit that description today — those with 85 or more years remaining and at least ten resales in the period: Jadescape, Thomson Impressions, Sky Vue, The Panorama and Thomson Three. Their median resale price is **S$2,165 per square foot**.

So: starting from S$2,165, how fast must District 20 compound for you to sell at your own entry price after roughly 6% of round-trip costs — stamp duty going in, agent commission coming out?

| Entry price | District 20 must compound at | over nine years |
|---|---|---|
| S$2,600 psf | **2.7% a year** | just to break even |
| S$2,900 psf | **4.0% a year** | just to break even |
| S$3,100 psf | **4.8% a year** | just to break even |

That is the whole decision in one line, and it is a function of a price list nobody has seen.

For scale, and this matters more than it looks: **there is no single long-run rate to compare it against.** URA's index has compounded at 1.8% a year over thirty years, 4.7% over twenty, 2.8% over fifteen, 4.6% over ten and 6.1% over five. Whoever picks the window picks the answer — which is [exactly the trap that makes the new-launch-versus-resale argument unwinnable](/new-launch-or-resale-who-picked-the-dates/). Nearer at hand, the District 20 resale median sits 4.7% above where it sat over the preceding twelve months — a median of what traded, so a shift in which flats sold moves it too — while the rest-of-central segment as a whole fell 1.2% in the second quarter of 2026, the weakest of the three segments.

The point is not which of those is right. It is that at the bottom of a price band the market can disappoint and you are still whole, and at the top it must beat most of its own history for nine consecutive years with nothing to spare. Same building, same view, same school — the risk is set entirely by the number that ends up on the list.

## What this method cannot tell you

Four things, and they are load-bearing.

**The exit comparable is an assumption.** Using today's modern District 20 leasehold price as a 2035 comparable assumes a nine-year hold and assumes the new-build premium fades to nothing by then. Whether it fades, and how fast, cannot be measured on free public data — following one unit from purchase to resale needs address-level records, which cost S$1,960 a year. Anyone confident on this point is guessing.

**Eight pairs is eight pairs.** All state land, all awarded 2022 to 2024, and measured at today's asking rather than launch-day pricing. A collective sale is also a different animal from a state tender, priced in a private negotiation against a reserve rather than a sealed bid.

**Medians hide units.** A district median mixes vintage, floor, stack, condition and renovation. It is the right benchmark for "how far above the neighbourhood is this" and the wrong one for "is this particular unit worth it".

**Supply here is a local question, not a national one.** Roughly 34,000 unsold new private homes nationally sounds like a headwind, but [that stock is concentrated in a handful of districts](/unsold-homes-which-districts/) and District 20 is one of five with no active launch in the latest monthly file at all. What this corridor faces is later rather than now: a large project's own units eventually reaching resale together.

## The boring version, all of it free

None of this requires paying anyone, and all of it can be done before you walk in.

**Ask whether the land rate is quoted before or after betterment charges and any lease premium.** If nobody can say, the figure is not usable.

**Get the land multiple from recent pairs, not from the rule of thumb.** Awarded tenders and the monthly developer-sales file are both public; match them on the developer's own name.

**Look up your size band, not the district median.** The gap on a 700 square foot flat and a 1,300 square foot flat in the same building are different numbers.

**Do the same-money swap.** Your budget divided by each nearby building's median price per square foot, and check a unit that size actually exists there.

**Compute your own break-even rate.** Entry price, times about 1.06, divided by today's comparable, then the ninth root, minus one. If that is higher than what you honestly believe the area will do, the building is fine and the price is not.

**Then test affordability at the 4% floor, not the package rate.** Banks stress-test the instalment at 4% even while packages sit near 1.5%, and the loan must run no more than 30 years *and* finish by age 65 to reach the full 75% loan-to-value. On a S$2.4 million purchase that is about S$693,000 of cash and CPF upfront and roughly S$15,600 of monthly household income with no other debt — and a buyer aged 42 is capped at 23 years, which lifts that income requirement by around a sixth. If those numbers do not clear comfortably, nothing else on this page matters.

The method does not produce a verdict, and it is not supposed to. It produces one honest sentence — *the market has to do this much, for this long, for me to break even* — and then hands you the only part that was ever yours: whether that is a bet you want, in a home you want, for as long as you would actually live in it.

#### Where our numbers are soft

- **The land multiple is eight pairs.** All are state land sales, all awarded between 2022 and 2024, and the launch side is each project's *current* median asking price, not its launch-day price — developers generally raise prices as a project sells through. Both sides of the Thomson Reserve comparison are today's prices, so the comparison is like for like, but the ratio should not be read as a developer's margin.
- **The multiple embeds market movement.** Between award and today the index rose; on a 24-month lag that is 6.5%. Our deflated median of about 2.16× applies the same two-year adjustment to every pair, which understates the drift for the four older sites — so the true drift-adjusted median is somewhat lower than 2.16×, and still above the old rule.
- **The S$714 figure is our arithmetic, not the agent's.** It divides S$810 million by site area times plot ratio. Gross floor area definitions vary at the margin — balcony and bonus allowances among them — so treat it as approximate. The direction is what matters: the quoted rate is materially higher than the headline sale price implies, and it says so.
- **The three illustrative prices are ours.** S$2,600, S$2,900 and S$3,100 were chosen to straddle what the observed multiples imply on this land cost. They are not a quote, a forecast or a valuation, and no marketing price list is reproduced here. When the real list appears, every table above should be re-run on it.
- **The nine-year hold and the fading premium are assumptions**, not findings, and the break-even table is only as good as they are. Six per cent of round-trip cost covers stamp duty in and agent commission out; it excludes legal fees, renovation, and every month of maintenance and property tax in between.
- **Size-band medians rest on subsets of 345 resales.** Our pipeline suppresses a band's percentiles below fifteen transactions, so every band shown clears that, but none is large.
- **The lease comparison is an association, not a decay curve.** Buildings differ in age, quality, finish and location as well as lease, and no attempt is made here to separate those.
- **Rent in the construction gap is computed from the district's current gross yield**, applied to today's resale prices. Rents change, the construction period is unannounced, and this cost is zero for anyone not renting through it.
