Picture a couple in their fifties. They bought a condo years ago, the mortgage is finally paid off, and the children have moved out. The place is worth far more than they paid for it, and two of the four bedrooms have not been slept in since 2019.

They have had the same conversation more than once. What if we sold it, moved into a flat, and kept the difference?

Until last week, that plan had a fifteen-month hole in the middle. You had to sell the condo **first**. Then wait fifteen months. Only then could you buy a flat on the open market. Fifteen months of renting somewhere in between, with the money parked and the furniture in storage. Most people did the sums, looked at the rent, and stayed put.

On 28 July 2026, the government scrapped that waiting rule.

## What changed

The Ministry of National Development and HDB announced that if you own private property, you can now buy a resale flat straight away. No waiting.

Two conditions come attached, and they matter more than the headline. The flat has to be one you buy **on the open market from another owner**, rather than a new subsidised flat from HDB. And you cannot use an **HDB housing loan** to buy it — you pay with cash, CPF, or a bank loan.

The reason given was that flat prices have cooled. HDB's official price tracker fell 0.1% in the first three months of 2026 and another 0.3% in the three months after that, following more than a year of slowing growth.

## The part that actually changes your life

The fifteen months is the headline. The order of events is the real news.

Before, it was sell, wait, buy. Now you can buy the flat while you still own the condo, move across once, and sell the condo afterwards. No renting in between. No storage. One move instead of two.

There is a catch, and it is the thing to understand before anything else. **You now have six months from the day the flat purchase completes to sell your condo.** That is not a guideline. It is a deadline.

## Why a deadline is scarier than a wait

A waiting period is annoying. You know the date, nothing surprises you, and the cost is rent.

A deadline is different, because two bills sit behind this one.

### The tax that gets waived, then unwaived

For the months when you own both the condo and the flat, you own two properties. Buying a second property normally means paying Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty — **20% if you are a Singapore citizen, 30% for a permanent resident**.

The tax office does not make you pay it upfront, because HDB already requires you to sell the condo within six months. So it is waived from the start.

Waived is not the same as gone. It is waived **on condition** that you actually sell in time. On a S$650,000 flat, a citizen who misses that deadline faces a **S$130,000** bill that did not exist the day before. Six months is not long to sell a property in a market that is currently soft.

### The tax on being forced to sell

If you bought your condo fairly recently, there is a second one. Selling any home within four years of buying it triggers Seller's Stamp Duty — **16% in year one, then 12%, 8% and 4%**. That four-year window was lengthened in July 2025.

So if you bought in 2024, and the six-month clock forces your sale in 2026, you pay it. The rule change did not create this tax. It created the situation where somebody else's calendar decides when you pay it.

Neither bill is a reason to stay put. Both are reasons to do the arithmetic before you commit rather than after.

### The surprise almost everyone gets wrong

Look at the CPF line.

If you used CPF to buy your home, and most people did, that money does not become yours to spend when you sell. It goes back into your CPF account. And it does not go back alone — you also put back the interest that money **would have earned** had you left it sitting in CPF, at 2.5% a year, for every year you owned the place.

Use S$180,000 of CPF twelve years ago, and roughly S$62,000 of interest goes back with it.

In plain English: the price your agent quotes and the money that lands in your bank account are two very different numbers, and this is usually most of the gap. The money is not lost. It is still yours, it keeps earning interest, and you can put it towards the next home. You just cannot spend it on a holiday.

One exception worth knowing. If you are 55 or older, the refund first tops up your Retirement Account, and what is left in your Ordinary Account can be taken out in cash.

## Even if you never move, this is worth two minutes

Here is the part that applies to everyone, not only the couple with the empty bedrooms.

How easily you can undo a housing decision determines how big a housing decision you should make in the first place.

When getting out meant selling first, waiting fifteen months and renting in between, stretching for a bigger place was close to permanent. If the repayments started to hurt, most families simply absorbed it for years, because the exit was miserable.

The exit is quicker now, and for the first time you do not have to be homeless in the middle of it. That does not make stretching a good idea. It changes what happens if the stretch stops working — which is a different question, and a better one to answer before signing anything.

Which is really the question underneath every housing decision. How much of everything you own are you willing to lock inside the place you sleep, and what does it cost you to change your mind later?

## This might not be about you at all

Three situations where today's news changes nothing.

**You need an HDB loan.** The old rules still apply to you: a separate 30-month wait after selling private property, which also covers new flats from HDB and Executive Condominiums. The change opened exactly one door, and that is not the one you are standing at.

**The flat you like is old.** CPF has rules about short leases. You cannot use CPF at all on a flat with under 20 years left, and to use it fully the lease has to cover the youngest buyer to age 95. Below that you get a reduced amount, worked out by a formula CPF does not publish — which is why the calculator above does not guess at it. Check your specific flat on CPF's own calculator.

**You do not actually need the money.** Freeing up S$900,000 only helps if that money has somewhere useful to go. Cash sitting in the bank earning less than the home it came from is a move that looked clever on paper and was not.

## Three boring things worth doing first

None of these cost anything, and all of them are cheaper than finding out later.

Pull up your CPF statement and find the real refund figure — what you have used, plus the interest owed back. Not an estimate. The actual number.

Check the date you bought your current place. If it was less than four years ago, work out what Seller's Stamp Duty would cost you.

And be honest about the loan. If your plan quietly assumes an HDB loan, the rule that changed last week is not the rule that was stopping you.
