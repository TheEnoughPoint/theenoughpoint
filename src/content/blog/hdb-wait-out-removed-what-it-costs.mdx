Picture a couple in their fifties. The mortgage on the condo is finally paid off, the children have moved out, and two of the four bedrooms have not been slept in since 2019.

They have had the same conversation more than once. What if we sold it, bought a flat, and kept the difference?

Until last week there was a good reason not to. You had to sell the condo **first**, then wait fifteen months before you could buy a flat on the open market — fifteen months of renting somewhere in between, with the money parked and the furniture in storage. On 28 July 2026 the government scrapped that waiting rule. You can now buy the flat first and sell the condo afterwards, so long as you are not using an HDB housing loan to do it.

Which brings the couple to the question that actually matters, and the one almost everyone gets wrong.

## How much would they actually keep?

Say the condo sells for S$1,700,000 and the flat costs S$650,000. The difference is a million dollars. That is the number people carry around in their heads.

## Does the CPF part actually matter?

Look at the CPF line, then consider ignoring it.

If you used CPF to buy your home, that money goes back into your CPF account when you sell — along with the interest it **would have earned** had you left it sitting there, at 2.5% a year. Use S$180,000 twelve years ago and around S$62,000 of interest goes back with it.

In the example above, that changes nothing at all. The S$242,080 goes back into CPF and comes straight out again to pay for the flat. Cash in hand is the same either way. Anyone telling you the CPF refund "eats your profit" has not done the arithmetic.

It starts to matter in two situations.

**You are trading down a long way.** If you have used a lot of CPF and buy something much cheaper, the refund is bigger than the new place can absorb, and the remainder stays in CPF. Someone who put S$600,000 of CPF into their home and buys a S$400,000 flat ends up with roughly S$400,000 sitting there — still theirs, still earning 2.5% — but not the money they pictured spending.

**You are under 55.** Then you cannot take it out at all. From 55, once your Retirement Account is topped up, what remains in the Ordinary Account can be withdrawn in cash.

So the question is not really how much you make on the move. It is how much of it you can actually touch, and when.

## And one deadline worth respecting

Buying before you sell has a condition attached. You have **six months** from getting the keys to sell the condo.

Miss it, and the stamp duty you avoided by moving in this order becomes payable — around **S$130,000** on a S$650,000 flat if you are a citizen. There is a second sting if you bought your current place less than four years ago: selling inside that window costs 16%, 12%, 8% or 4% in Seller's Stamp Duty depending on the year, so the deadline can force a sale that carries its own bill.

Six months is not long to sell a property in a soft market. Worth knowing before you commit, rather than after.

## Before you do anything

Pull up your CPF statement and find the real number — what you have used, plus the interest owed back. It takes two minutes and it is the figure that decides whether this move does what you think it does.

Then ask the question underneath the whole thing: how much of everything you own do you want locked inside the place you sleep?
