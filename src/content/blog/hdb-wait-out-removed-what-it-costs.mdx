If you own private property and have ever run the numbers on selling it for a resale flat, you already know the door back down was never locked. It was slow.

Since 30 September 2022, a private property owner had to dispose of that property **first**, then wait fifteen months, and only then buy a non-subsidised resale flat. Fifteen months housed somewhere else, at your own expense, with the capital parked and the family in a rental. That friction was the policy. It was not a tax and it was not a ban — it was a delay expensive enough that most households did not bother.

On 28 July 2026, that requirement was removed with immediate effect.

## What actually changed

The Ministry of National Development and HDB announced that private residential property owners and former owners buying a **non-subsidised HDB resale flat without an HDB housing loan** are no longer subject to the 15-month wait-out period. The reason given was that resale prices have cooled. The announcement points to the Resale Price Index falling 0.1% in the first quarter of 2026 and a further 0.3% in the second, after five consecutive quarters of slower or no growth. Computed from the same published index, annual growth ran at 10.4% in 2022 and 2.9% in 2025.

The headline is the fifteen months. The more consequential change is to the **order of events**.

Under the old rule the sequence was sell, wait, buy. Under the new one you may buy the flat while still owning your private property — and then you must dispose of it, in Singapore or overseas, **within six months of the resale flat's completion**.

That is not a smaller version of the same rule. It is a different rule, and it moves the risk from your calendar to your balance sheet.

## A deadline has teeth a waiting period never had

A waiting period is inconvenient. You know the date, nothing surprises you, and the cost is rent and disruption. A deadline is a different animal, because two separate charges are now sitting behind it.

**The first is the stamp duty that was waived on condition.** When you buy the flat while still holding private property, you are buying a second residential property, and Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty would ordinarily apply — 20% for a Singapore Citizen, 30% for a Singapore PR buying a second property. Because HDB requires disposal within six months, the tax authority grants the remission **upfront**, so you pay at your post-disposal count instead. The relief is real. It is also conditional. On a S$650,000 flat, a citizen who does not complete that disposal in time is looking at a six-figure liability that did not exist the day before the deadline passed.

**The second is the duty on the sale you are now obliged to make.** Seller's Stamp Duty applies to residential property sold within four years of purchase, at 16%, 12%, 8% and 4% by year — a schedule that was lengthened and raised in July 2025. If you bought your private property recently, the six-month disposal clock can land you inside that window and force the sale anyway. The rule change did not create this charge; it created the situation where you may have to pay it on someone else's timetable.

Neither of these is a reason not to move. Both are reasons to know the numbers before the option to change your mind expires.

The line that surprises people is the CPF refund. When you sell a property you funded with CPF, the money you took out goes back in — along with the interest it would have earned had it stayed there, at the Ordinary Account rate of 2.5% a year. That is not a penalty and it is not lost. It is simply not *cash*. In plain English: the number your agent quotes and the number that reaches your bank account are different numbers, and this is usually most of the gap.

If you are 55 or over, the refund first tops up your Retirement Account toward the Full Retirement Sum, and what remains in the Ordinary Account can be withdrawn. Below 55, it stays in CPF and can be used for the next property, but not spent.

## Why a faster exit changes the entry

Here is the part that outlasts the news cycle.

The cost of reversing a housing decision is what determines how large a housing decision you can sensibly make. When the way back down meant selling first, waiting fifteen months and renting in between, stretching for a larger private home was close to a one-way commitment: if the mortgage became uncomfortable, the exit was slow enough that most households simply absorbed the discomfort for years.

The exit is now faster and, for the first time, does not require you to be homeless in the middle. That does not make the stretch wiser. It changes what happens when the stretch stops working — which is a different question from whether to stretch at all, and a better one to have answered in advance.

Which returns to the question underneath every housing decision: how much of your net worth are you willing to keep locked inside the place you sleep, and what does it cost you to change your mind about it later?

## Where this changes nothing

For a large share of readers, this announcement is not their announcement.

If you need an **HDB housing loan**, nothing has changed for you: the separate 30-month wait after disposing of private property still applies, and it applies to subsidised flats and Executive Condominium purchases too. The removal covers exactly one route — a non-subsidised resale flat, bought without an HDB loan.

If the flat you have in mind has a short remaining lease, CPF rules bind before anything else does. CPF savings can only be used where the remaining lease is at least 20 years, and full usage requires the lease to cover the youngest buyer to age 95. Below that the amount is pro-rated by a formula CPF does not publish, which is why the calculator above does not guess at it.

And a faster door is not an argument for walking through it. Freed capital is only worth freeing if it has somewhere to go. Capital sitting in a bank account earning less than the home it came from is a move that looked profitable on a spreadsheet and was not.

## The boring part that actually matters

Before any of this becomes a decision, three unglamorous steps do most of the work. Pull your CPF statement and find the actual refund figure, principal and accrued interest, rather than estimating it. Check the purchase date on your private property against the four-year Seller's Stamp Duty window. And confirm whether your financing plan depends on an HDB loan, because if it does, the rule that changed today is not the rule that binds you.

None of that costs anything. All of it is cheaper than discovering the answer six months after completion.
