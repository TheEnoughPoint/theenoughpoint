**In December 2013 we built a spreadsheet to settle an argument with ourselves: which credit card was actually worth carrying?**

It was not sophisticated. Two tabs. One listed the cards of the day and what each earned per dollar. The other priced Singapore Airlines redemptions — São Paulo, London, Seoul — against the cash fares for the same seats, worked out what a mile was worth in each case, and multiplied that by each card's earn rate to produce a single comparable number: **the effective rebate**. Cents back per dollar spent, which is the only unit in which a miles card and a cashback card can honestly be set beside each other.

The sheet worked. It picked the card, the card earned the miles, and the miles turned into six business-class seats to Shanghai one December, the family home from Hong Kong a fortnight later with two of them in First, four seats to Cape Town and back this June, and a Suite to Sydney that flies in October. The whole ledger is further down, to the mile and to the cent.

It also contained a mistake. A flattering one, and the same one almost every miles comparison on the internet still makes. We will come to it, because correcting it is most of what this piece is for.

## The formula that has not changed

Here it is, and it is the whole of the technical content:

> **(the cash fare you would genuinely have paid − the taxes you still pay in cash) ÷ miles surrendered = value per mile**

Then, to make it comparable with a cashback card:

> **value per mile (cents) × miles earned per dollar = effective rebate (%)**

The second line is an identity rather than a claim — cents per mile multiplied by miles per dollar gives cents per dollar, which is a percentage by construction. Nothing to argue with.

The first line is where every disagreement lives, and it is worth saying plainly that we did not invent it. It is the standard framing in the Singapore miles community, set out most clearly by [The MileLion](https://milelion.com/airlines/what-is-the-value-of-a-mile/), Aaron Wong's site, which has been the reference work on this subject here for well over a decade. His two-sided version is worth stating as he does: there is a price you should be *willing to pay* to acquire a mile, and a value you should *insist on getting* when you spend one — he puts those at roughly 1.5 cents and 1.7 cents respectively, with the gap standing in for the effort involved. Much of what follows is our arithmetic laid over his framework, and where we have leaned on his reporting for a programme rule or a fee, we have said so.

## The one-cent exit — and why it is a ceiling, not a floor

Start at the bottom, because it settles more than people expect — though not in the direction most write-ups claim, and we had this the wrong way round ourselves until we checked it properly.

From **1 July 2025**, Singapore Airlines harmonised the redemption rate across its own ecosystem: Miles+Cash on SIA and Scoot tickets, Kris+ at partner merchants, KrisShop, and Pelago bookings all pay **100 miles = S$1**. One cent a mile, no seat to hunt for, no date to work around, no availability to wait on. Before that the rate wandered between roughly 0.67 and 0.95 cents depending on which door you used; now it does not.

The tempting conclusion is that a mile is therefore never worth less than a cent. **It is not true, and the reason is worth understanding**, because it is the difference between a rate and a value.

One cent a mile is what the airline *credits* you. What you *receive* depends on what the thing you bought was really worth. The MileLion's worked example is the one to remember: a pair of Sony headphones priced **S$507.76 on Amazon and S$559 on KrisShop**. Pay the KrisShop price in miles at the official rate and you have spent 55,900 miles on S$507.76 of headphones — **0.91 cents a mile**, not one. The rate was honoured exactly. The value was not there to begin with.

So the honest statement is narrower, and it splits by door:

- **Kris+**, where partner merchants charge their normal prices, comes closest to a genuine cent.
- **Miles+Cash against a ticket you were buying anyway** is close too — but not clean, because an award-discounted ticket earns you fewer miles than the cash fare would have, and that forgone earn is a cost.
- **KrisShop** is where one cent becomes a **ceiling**: it is the most you can get, and you get it only where the sticker matches the market. Price-controlled goods come close; anything the shop can mark up does not.

Which means the one-cent exit is better described as **the rate you get for doing nothing** than as a floor beneath you. It still does real work in this article — it is the benchmark every redemption should be measured against, and it is why a 1.4 mpd card is worth *roughly* 1.4% even if you never book an award seat. But treat it as a soft benchmark that leaks a little at the edges, not a guarantee.

If anything, that makes the rest of this piece matter more. **A soft benchmark is a weaker reason to hold miles, and a stronger reason to have somewhere specific to spend them.** Which sets the question the rest of this piece answers: what has to be true for a mile to be worth several times that?

## Why the front of the aircraft

The answer is structural, and it is visible without knowing a single fare.

Singapore Airlines publishes its award chart. That chart prices cabins on a ladder — and the ladder is remarkably flat.

<CabinLadder />

Two to two and a half times the miles for business. About three times for a Suite. Those are not our estimates; they are division applied to a published document.

One qualification belongs here rather than in a footnote: **the ladder is flat in price and steep in availability.** Business Saver seats are released in far smaller numbers than economy Saver seats, so the cheap rung of the premium cabin is the hardest one in the programme to actually stand on. The price is published; the seat is not promised. Our own Cape Town booking further down is what that looks like when it goes against you.

Now hold that against the other ladder — the cash fare table — which is not published in advance, is remade daily by revenue management, and on a long-haul route separates economy from business by a great deal more than 2.5 times. We are deliberately not putting a number on that gap, because it is not ours to assert: it moves by route, season and how far ahead you look, and the only fare that belongs in your calculation is the one you actually saw on the day.

But the *mechanism* is not in dispute, and it is the reason this article exists. **The airline sells you the front cabin at a steep premium in dollars and a modest premium in miles.** A mile does not know which cabin it is buying. Point it at the cabin where the two ladders diverge most, and it is worth multiples of what it is worth pointed anywhere else.

This is also why the intuitive move — spending miles on a cheap economy ticket because it feels thrifty — is usually the worst available use of them. A discounted economy fare is the one seat the airline is already selling cheaply for cash. Redeeming against it means surrendering a currency with a one-cent exit to buy something the market has already marked down.

Our 2013 sheet showed exactly this, on its own numbers. Singapore to London: at the full-flexibility economy fare, a mile came out at **3.24 cents**; at the same route's deepest economy sale fare, **0.91 cents** — below the one-cent exit that exists today. In business, **4.67 cents**. The same mile, five times the value, decided entirely by what it was pointed at.

## The haircut, which is the honest part

Now the mistake.

Our 2013 sheet valued each redemption at the published fare of the cabin flown. For the London business seat it used a **S$7,366** flexible fare, deducted the award taxes, and returned 4.67 cents a mile and a **5.60%** effective rebate on a 1.2-mile-per-dollar card. Impressive figures. Also, in a real sense, fictional — because we were never going to spend S$7,366 on that ticket. Nobody in the family was. Had the miles not existed, we would have flown economy, or not gone.

The MileLion puts the correction more sharply than we would dare to: you cannot claim to value your miles at some high figure *unless you would genuinely have paid cash for that ticket*. That is the discipline the whole field is short of. A valuation measured against a fare you would never pay is not a valuation. It is the brochure.

So the honest numerator is a counterfactual: **what would you actually have spent, had the miles not existed?** For most people, most of the time, the answer for a premium cabin sits somewhere between "the economy fare I would really have bought" and "the business fare I enjoyed" — and where it sits is a genuine, personal judgement, not a number anyone can look up.

That is what the slider in this tool is for. It is the difference between a miles calculation you can defend and one that merely flatters you.

<MileValueTool />

Drag the haircut to zero on any of those presets and watch the answer collapse. That collapse is the honest case *against* miles for a household that would never buy a premium seat with cash — and it deserves to be stated as plainly as the case for. If you would fly economy regardless, then a business redemption is a genuine pleasure and a poor financial argument, and the cashback card was probably the better instrument all along.

The case *for* miles survives that test only for people in one of two positions: those who would genuinely have bought the front cabin with cash, and those for whom the upgrade is worth a real, sizeable fraction of its price even though they would not have paid the whole of it. Both are legitimate. Neither is universal, and the difference between them is worth more than every card-selection tip ever written.

## The hurdle your card has to clear

With value per mile settled, the card question becomes trivial arithmetic.

A miles card beats a flat cashback card when

> **value per mile (cents) × miles per dollar ≥ cashback rate (%)**

which rearranges to the only number you need to carry around:

> **breakeven = cashback rate ÷ miles per dollar**

At the **1.7%** currently available on the best uncapped flat-cashback card in Singapore, that gives:

| If your card earns | It needs this per mile to match 1.7% cashback |
| --- | --- |
| 1.2 mpd — entry general spend | **1.42 cents** |
| 1.4 mpd — higher general spend | **1.21 cents** |
| 2.4 mpd — overseas spend | **0.71 cents** |
| 4.0 mpd — capped bonus category | **0.43 cents** |

Read that table against the one-cent exit and a hard conclusion falls out: **a general-spending miles card earning 1.2 to 1.4 miles per dollar does not beat cashback on that rate alone.** It needs a redemption at 1.21 to 1.42 cents a mile merely to draw level — so if the miles end up in KrisShop or a Miles+Cash ticket, the cashback card would have paid more, and paid it without the annual fee or the three-year expiry clock.

One caveat on the bottom row, because it is the one most easily misread: **4 miles per dollar is a rate on a slice of your spending, not on your spending.** Those cards cap the bonus rate at roughly S$1,000 a month each, so that line describes what happens inside the cap and nothing outside it. A household putting everything through one card lands far closer to the 1.2 and 1.4 rows, and the blended rate across all of your spending is the only one that decides whether cashback would have paid more.

Above 2 miles per dollar the hurdle drops below one cent, and the miles card wins even if you never book an award seat at all. This is why the modern Singapore approach has moved away from a single general-spend card toward bonus-category cards at 4 miles per dollar, capped monthly — a shift The MileLion's [2026 strategy](https://milelion.com/2026/05/11/the-milelions-2026-credit-card-strategy/) documents in detail, along with its real cost: a fistful of cards, monthly caps to track, and points scattered across currencies that each want a **S$25 to S$30** conversion fee to move.

Which lands on the sentence this whole section exists to produce. **The miles game is not free money; it is paid work.** The pay is the gap between your effective rebate and the cashback you declined. Decide whether you want the job before optimising how you do it.

## Four doors into the same cabin

One more thing stands between a chart price and a seat, and it is the part that turns a tidy calculation into a hobby.

<AwardTypes />

Nearly all of the value described in this article lives in **Saver** awards — the cheapest rung, and the scarcest. That is not a footnote; it is the cost of entry. Saver space on a popular route in a school holiday goes when the booking window opens, eleven and a half months ahead, which means the six-seat family redemption is not a booking, it is a diary entry made a year in advance. Everything above Saver — Advantage at roughly 80% more on average, Access priced dynamically above that — is the airline selling you certainty in exchange for the value you came for.

We can put a precise price on that, because we paid it. Four of us flew to Cape Town on **SQ 478 on Thursday 4 June this year** — one aeroplane, one date, four seats in the same cabin. Two of those seats were booked at **Saver, 56,500 miles each**. The other two were booked a week later, once it was clear no more Saver space would open, at **Advantage: 103,500 miles each**.

**Identical seats on an identical flight, 83% apart in price.** Not a better cabin, not a better date, not a more flexible ticket in any way that mattered to us — the entire difference was that two seats existed at the cheap price and two did not. Those two Advantage seats cost 94,000 miles more than the Saver pair, which is most of another long-haul business ticket, spent on nothing but arriving in the same seats on the same day.

That is what scarcity costs, and it is the number to hold against every cheerful cents-per-mile figure you will read, including the ones in this article.

One boundary on all of this, stated plainly: **this is a Singapore Airlines piece.** KrisFlyer also books Star Alliance partners on a separate chart, and those redemptions carry a complication SIA's own awards do not — several partners levy fuel surcharges that KrisFlyer passes straight through to you, which can consume a large part of a redemption before you have flown a metre. That deserves its own article and this one does not attempt it. An Access redemption at 2 cents a mile is a different financial object from a Saver redemption at 5, and on the day you book, the airline decides which one you are being offered.

## The chart is not a promise

And now the risk that no amount of optimisation removes.

<Devaluation />

Nine months ago the price of these seats changed, and every mile already earned was worth less the next morning. Note where the increases concentrated: the **Advantage** awards that are actually available, and the zone that had been the chart's best value. Note also, in fairness, that economy Saver awards in the nearer zones got *cheaper* — the revision had a direction, and the direction was to charge more for the front of the aircraft, which is precisely where this article says the value is.

The longer view is more sobering, and we can draw it from our own file. That December 2013 workbook priced a **Singapore–London business return at 136,000 miles**, including the 15% discount then given for booking online. The same return today, at Saver, is **217,000 miles**.

There are two honest ways to read that, and they belong together rather than whichever one flatters the argument. **What a customer actually pays** went from 136,000 to 217,000 — up **59.6%**, or **3.8% a year** — because the online discount was itself withdrawn along the way. **What the chart charges** went from 160,000, the undiscounted 2013 price, to 217,000 — up **35.6%**, or **2.4% a year**. The first is the number your miles balance experiences; the second is the cleaner like-for-like. Neither is small, and both point the same way.

Call that what it is: an inflation rate, on a currency you cannot hedge, set by the party that issues it, disclosed after the fact. It is not a reason to avoid miles. It is a decisive reason to treat a miles balance as a **perishable good rather than savings** — and it stacks on top of the programme's own three-year expiry, which independently guarantees that miles held too long become miles worth nothing.

The practical conclusion is unglamorous and, we think, the single most valuable habit in the whole hobby: **earn toward a booking you can already name, and book it.** Miles held are exposed to a chart that will be revised. Miles booked are a seat.

## What we actually redeemed

Here is the ledger, from the confirmation emails rather than from memory: nine bookings, four trips, every figure as the airline billed it.

<RedemptionLedger />

**One and a half million miles, and S$5,215 of tax, for thirty one-way journeys in the front of the aircraft.** That is the whole record. A few things in it are worth pulling out, because each one corrects something the brochures imply.

**The taxes are not a rounding error.** Across the ledger they came to S$5,215.35 in cash, and they are wildly uneven: the Johannesburg–Singapore sector alone accounted for **S$2,236.68 — 43% of all the tax we paid across nine bookings**, for a single flight. The same trip in the opposite direction, Singapore to Cape Town, cost **S$65.20 a head**; coming home from Johannesburg cost **S$559.17 a head**, eight and a half times as much, for the same four people in the same cabin. Award tickets are not free tickets. Departure taxes are a fact about the airport you leave from, and on some routes they will quietly consume a large part of what the miles saved you.

**The chart moves under your feet, and you can watch it happen here.** The Shanghai seats were booked in January 2024 at 43,000 miles each — the Zone 5 business Saver price of the day. The Sydney Suite was booked on 6 November 2025 at 178,500 miles, which is the Zone 9 First Advantage price on the chart that had taken effect **five days earlier**. Both were correct on the day. Neither would be correct now.

**And the seat you get is the seat that exists.** The Cape Town pair above is the clearest thing in this ledger: two of us at 56,500 miles, two at 103,500, same flight, same day, because Saver space ran out between one booking and the next.

Two honest notes. First, **we have deliberately not published a cents-per-mile figure for any of these**, and we are not going to. Doing so would require us to state what we would have paid in cash for seats we did not buy, and that is a judgement about our own household, not a fact about the world — precisely the move this article spent a section warning against. The miles and taxes above are exact, they are loaded into the tool as presets, and the counterfactual is yours to supply.

Second, the part that actually generalises is not financial at all. The six-seat and four-seat family redemptions were possible only because somebody sat down eleven months in advance and held the date. That is a planning discipline, not a money technique, and it is the thing that separates people who fly on miles from people who accumulate them.

## So what do you do?

Five steps, and the first two settle it for most people.

1. **Answer the counterfactual honestly, before anything else.** Would you, with your own money, buy a premium-cabin long-haul ticket? If the answer is a clean no, and will stay no, then the case for miles over cashback is weak and the rest of this is a hobby you may still enjoy — just not one that should be filed under money management.

2. **Work out your breakeven and check it against the one-cent exit.** Cashback rate divided by miles per dollar. If your card earns 1.2 to 1.4 on general spend, you need a real redemption above roughly 1.2 to 1.4 cents a mile to have been right — and cashing out at KrisShop will not get you there.

3. **Point the miles at the cabin where the two ladders diverge.** The award chart is flat across cabins; the fare table is not. That gap is the whole return, and it is largest on long-haul premium seats — which is also, unhelpfully, where Saver space is scarcest.

4. **Book, do not accumulate.** Name the trip, then earn toward it. A balance is exposed to a chart revision and a three-year clock; a confirmed seat is neither.

5. **Then price what you actually did**, with the haircut applied, using the tool above. If the honest answer is under a cent, the programme is telling you something useful, and it is not a criticism of the trip.
