Suppose you had been right about Korea.

You watched the AI and memory trade building through 2024, decided Korean equities were the way to play it, and you were correct. Between July 2024 and July 2026, the main Korea ETF that foreign investors use rose about **144%**.

Now suppose you had wanted more of it, and bought a fund promising three times the daily move of the same market. You would have made 286%, not the 431% that "three times" implies.

And if you had held that fund for five years instead of two, you would have done worse than the people who simply bought the ordinary index and forgot about it.

Those are not our estimates. They come from the standardised returns each fund publishes, on the same date, 30 June 2026 — where the leveraged fund shows **13.8% a year over five years against the plain fund's 19.1%**, and 14.4% against 16.7% over ten.

Triple the exposure. Triple the drawdowns. A lower return than owning the index outright.

Nothing went wrong. Two things were quietly eating the difference the whole time.

## The first: a machine that buys high and sells low, every day

The fund promises three times the move of the index **for a single day**. That is its own wording, printed above its own price. Not three times your year.

Here is why one day does not add up to one year.

You put in $100. To give you 3× exposure, the fund holds $300 of the market.

**Day one, the market rises 10%.** That $300 becomes $330, so you have gained $30. Your money is now $130 — and to keep you at 3×, the fund must hold $390. It buys another $60 of the market. *After it has gone up.*

**Day two, the market falls 10%.** But now the fall applies to $390, not $330. You lose $39. You are left with $91.

Meanwhile the index went up 10% and down 10%, which leaves it at 99 — down 1%.

Three times a 1% loss should be 3%. You lost **9%**. No fee was charged and nobody made a mistake. The fund simply had to buy more at the top of day one to keep its promise, and that larger position met the fall on day two.

That is the machine. Every day the market rises, it buys more. Every day the market falls, it sells. It is required to, because the promise is defined against whatever the fund is worth *today*. In a market that goes up and down — which is most markets — it is buying high and selling low, over and over, by design.

The Korea episode shows it happening. The 3× fund peaked on **1 June 2026**. The index it tracks kept climbing, and peaked on **18 June** — seventeen days later. The market went on making new highs the leveraged fund never saw again.

## The second: a borrowing bill you cannot see

The fund's published fee is 1.32% a year. That number is true and close to irrelevant.

To hold $300 of market for your $100, it has to get the other $200 somewhere. It rents it, through contracts with investment banks, and pays them a short-term interest rate plus a spread — around 4.5% a year in mid-2026, on the borrowed portion.

None of that is in the 1.32%. The fund's own fee agreement excludes "swap financing and related costs", then adds, in its own words: **"If these expenses were included, the expense ratio would be higher."**

How much higher is public record. Its regulatory filing for 30 April 2026 lists swaps with a face value of **US$3.86 billion** against **US$1.55 billion** of fund assets, at rates between 4.34% and 5.34%. That is roughly **US$176 million a year — about 11% of everything in the fund.**

So the real cost of holding is somewhere near **12% a year**, of which the published figure is about a tenth. Not a scandal. That is simply what borrowing costs. But it means the fund starts each year twelve points behind.

The number to watch is the last one. At 30% volatility — roughly a single-country market — the index must compound at about **13.5% a year, or 3.2% a quarter,** before a 3× holder makes anything at all. Below that, you lose money in a rising market.

## "But these are for short-term trading"

That defence is right, and much stricter than most people realise. The fund's own horizon is not "short term". It is **one day**.

Three months is not a rounding error against that. At 30% volatility, the daily rebalancing costs around 6.5% over a quarter and the borrowing adds roughly 2.6% more — so about **9% disappears in three months before the index does anything.**

The Korea episode settles it. The 3× fund fell **74%** from its June peak while the index fell **26%**, and that took about eight weeks. Anyone holding "just a quarter" held through the whole thing.

One honest complication, in the other direction. On the way down the machine works in reverse: as the fund shrinks it sells, so it carries less exposure into each subsequent fall. Three times a 26% drop would have been 79%, and the fund lost 74%. The daily reset is not simply "worse" — it is a **volatility cost, not a direction cost**. What it punishes is chop, and chop is what markets mostly do.

## The question worth sitting with

None of this makes these funds wrong. They do what the label says, and for someone holding a day or a week, the arithmetic above barely registers.

But it is worth being clear about which risk you are taking. If you borrow to invest, the danger is that the market falls and the debt does not — painful, and widely understood. That loan does not care what route the price took: down 20% then up 25% leaves you where you started. A daily-reset fund does care, because the rebalancing happens whether you like it or not, and every swing takes a bite on the way.

So the question is not whether you are right about the market. It is whether the thing you are holding can survive being right slowly.
