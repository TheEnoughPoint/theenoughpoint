On 27 May 2026, Korea did something no market had done before: it listed leveraged funds on **single stocks**. Two, specifically — Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the only names large enough to qualify. Each promised twice the daily move.

Korean savers understood the appeal immediately. Within about a fortnight the money in these products roughly doubled, from around US$3 billion at listing to close to US$9 billion. Roughly **92%** of the buying was retail. Not institutions. People.

Then the memory trade turned. Both stocks fell more than **20%** from their late-June highs inside about a week — a bear market in seven trading days, while Samsung was still reporting record profits. On 29 July the SK Hynix-linked leveraged funds fell **around a fifth in a single session**.

The regulator's response was to triple the minimum deposit required to buy them, from ₩10 million to ₩30 million, and the head of the financial supervisor publicly regretted how quickly they had been approved.

That is the story most people now have about leveraged funds. It is true, and it is half the picture. The other half is stranger, and more useful.

## First, what the ride actually looks like

Before any argument about whether these products are good or bad, look at what holding one does to you.

<LeverageDrawdownChart />

The 3× semiconductor fund is one of the great success stories in this category — 65.9% a year for a decade. It also spent **more than half of the last sixteen years down over 30%** from its own high, and **nearly a third of that time down over 50%**. Its worst fall was **90.5%**. The plain semiconductor fund, across exactly the same years, spent 3.7% of days that far underwater and never once halved.

Sit with what that means in practice. You put in S$50,000. Eighteen months later the statement says S$9,000, and everyone around you is explaining why semiconductors are finished. The published 65.9% belongs to whoever held through that screen — and held through it again, because it happened more than once.

**That is the mismatch.** Nobody buys a 2× fund on Samsung expecting to watch it halve, twice, while being right about Samsung. They expect roughly twice the ride they already understand. The gap between those two expectations is where the damage happens — not usually in the arithmetic, but in the entirely human decision to sell at the bottom of a fall that was never in the plan.

If you use a stop-loss, the problem inverts and gets worse: a level that would rarely trigger on the ordinary stock gets hit routinely on a 2× or 3× version of it. You are closed out of a position that later recovers — right about the company, stopped out anyway.

## You did not need a Korean account to be in this

Singapore readers might reasonably file the Korean episode under someone else's problem. It is not.

Hong Kong lists leveraged products on the **same two stocks**, and most Singapore brokers can reach them. CSOP's SK Hynix 2× (**7709.HK**) held about **US$5.5 billion** at the end of July; its Samsung Electronics 2× (**7747.HK**) about US$1.17 billion. These are not obscure instruments.

Take the cleanest example first, and it is not the memory names. CSOP's **Nvidia 2× (7788.HK) is down 0.49% for the year to 30 June. Nvidia stock is up 7.29%.** You were right about the most talked-about company on earth, you bought the product designed to double it, and you finished slightly behind where you started.

The SK Hynix fund tells the other half. Bought in January and held, it returned roughly **798%** for the half-year, against about 307% for the stock — the trend rewarded it spectacularly. But it also reportedly peaked as Hong Kong's largest ETF at over US$20 billion in June before falling **70–80% within a month**, and on 31 July it moved **+67.7% in a single day**. Same fund, same year: enter in January and you multiplied your money; enter at the June peak and most of it went.

The issuer is admirably direct about why. From CSOP's own key facts statement:

> "This product is not intended for holding longer than one day… it is even possible that the Product will lose money over time while the Underlying Stock's performance increases or is flat."

And on cost, where the Hong Kong disclosure is far blunter than anything American issuers print: CSOP discloses swap and option costs of **up to 40% a year** of net assets on the SK Hynix product, and up to 35.2% on the Samsung one. Before the market does anything at all.

## And yet, on other markets, it worked

None of that makes these products a trap. Look at the same structure elsewhere.

<LeverageSpreadChart />

A holder of the 3× semiconductor fund ended the decade with roughly **seven times** what an ordinary semiconductor fund holder had. The 3× Nasdaq fund averaged **45.5% a year**. These are not near-misses, and anyone who tells you these products are simply a scam has not looked at them.

On other markets the same design did the opposite: 3× small caps and 3× Korea both left their holders with **less than the plain index** over the same period.

So the product is not the variable. The mechanism inside it is the same in both cases — and it is worth two minutes of your time, because it explains everything above.

## Why the same machine does both

The promise is three times the move of the index **for a single day**. Not three times your year.

Here is why one day does not add up to one year.

You put in $100. To give you 3× exposure, the fund holds $300 of the market.

**Day one, the market rises 10%.** That $300 becomes $330, so you have gained $30. Your money is now $130 — and to keep you at 3×, the fund must hold $390. It buys another $60 of the market. *After it has gone up.*

**Day two, the market falls 10%.** But now the fall applies to $390, not $330. You lose $39. You are left with $91.

Meanwhile the index went up 10% and down 10%, which leaves it at 99 — down 1%.

Three times a 1% loss should be 3%. You lost **9%**. No fee was charged and nobody made a mistake. The fund simply had to buy more at the top of day one to keep its promise, and that larger position met the fall on day two.

That is the machine. Every day the market rises, it buys more. Every day the market falls, it sells. It is required to, because the promise is defined against whatever the fund is worth *today*.

**Which explains both halves of the chart above.** In a market that climbs steadily, buying more after every up day is exactly the right move, and the machine hands you more than three times the index — that is the semiconductor and Nasdaq decade. In a market that lurches up and down without going anywhere, the same machine buys high and sells low, over and over, by design. That is small caps, and that is Korea.

You can watch it happen. In Korea this June the 3× country fund peaked on **1 June**; the index it tracks kept climbing and did not peak until **18 June**. Over those seventeen days the index rose a further 1.2% — but it travelled **59%** in daily moves to get there, and the fund, buying and selling into every one of them, lost **13.8%**.

<LeveragePeakChart />

One point in the machine's favour, since it is owed. On the way down it works in reverse: as the fund shrinks it sells, so it carries less exposure into each subsequent fall. Three times Korea's 28% drop would have been 85%, and the fund lost 76%. The daily reset is not simply a penalty — it is a **volatility cost, not a direction cost.** What it punishes is chop.

## Singapore has its own version, and it goes to seven times

None of this is a distant American problem.

The Singapore Exchange has listed **Daily Leverage Certificates** since 2017 — the first market in Asia to do so. They offer fixed daily leverage of **2, 3, 5 and 7 times**, long or short, on indices and on single stocks. The single-stock list includes American names like Apple, Nvidia and Tesla, Hong Kong names like Tencent and Alibaba, and Singapore's own: DBS, OCBC, UOB, Singtel, Singapore Airlines.

They work the same way, and the exchange says so plainly:

> "The performance of the underlying asset and the DLC is reset at the end of each trading day… Over the period of more than one day, the profits or losses are thus compounded."

To its credit, SGX publishes worked examples that include the uncomfortable case, and these certificates carry an **air bag** that halts trading on a sharp adverse move — though in the issuer's own words it then leaves you with "a reduced exposure… thereby reducing its ability to recover losses" if the market turns back your way.

**What stands between an ordinary investor and 7× leverage on a single bank stock is one form.** These are classified as Specified Investment Products, so a broker must run a Customer Account Review first. You pass by holding a relevant qualification, or three years of relevant work experience, or six such trades in the past three years — or by passing a free online quiz.

Worth knowing: on 15 May 2026 the Monetary Authority concluded a consultation that would **remove the mandatory financial-advice requirement** for complex products, replacing it with alerts and learning modules. The legislation has not been drafted yet, so today's rules still stand. But the direction is toward fewer gates, not more — which is exactly the setting in which Korea listed two single-stock leveraged funds, watched retail buy nine billion dollars of them, and then tripled the deposit requirement afterwards.

## The question is not the product

Leveraged funds are not a scam and they are not free money. On the right market, over the right stretch, they have delivered outcomes ordinary funds cannot approach. On the wrong one they have quietly lost to the index they track.

But that framing still puts the question in the wrong place. Even the funds that won demanded that their holders sit through 80% and 90% falls, more than once, without flinching. The return was real. Almost nobody was in a position to collect it.

The people who held the 3× semiconductor fund through the last decade were not smarter than the people who held 3× small caps. They were in a market that went one way — and they had the stomach, or the inattention, to stay put while it did.

So the question worth answering before you buy one is not whether you are right about the market, nor how long you intend to stay. It is a plainer one: **if this halves, and then halves again, what will you actually do?** Answer that honestly first, because the market will ask it.

#### Where our numbers are soft

**The drawdown study is evidence, not a law.** It covers sixteen years and a handful of funds, and those sixteen years contained one of the strongest bull runs in market history — which is precisely why the winning cases look as good as they do. Read it as a description of what this era did, not a forecast of the next.

The Korean episode is reported from Korean issuer notices, Korean financial press and market data. The asset and retail-share figures circulate in several outlets with small variations, so treat "roughly US$3 billion to roughly US$9 billion" and "roughly 92% retail" as well-supported approximations rather than audited numbers. The single-day fall in the SK Hynix-linked funds on 29 July is reported from exchange data via secondary outlets; the underlying stocks' fall of more than 20% from late-June highs is the more solidly sourced fact of the two.

Our figures also come from a small number of funds over particular windows, which is a fair thing to be suspicious of. The underlying relationship is neither ours nor new: Avellaneda and Zhang derived it formally and tested it across 56 leveraged funds, and independent work has checked it against more than a century of data across eight equity markets. More recent research argues the picture is subtler still — that outcomes turn on whether a market trends or mean-reverts, not on volatility alone, which is consistent with the two-sided result above.
