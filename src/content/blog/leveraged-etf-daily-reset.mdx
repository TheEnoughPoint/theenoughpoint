Every leveraged fund carries the same warning, printed by the people who sell it: not to be held for more than a day.

Almost nobody obeys it. So the useful question is not what the label says — it is what actually happened to people who ignored it. Ten years of published records answer that, and the answer is not the one either camp expects.

<LeverageSpreadChart />

A holder of the 3× semiconductor fund ended the decade with **seven times** what an ordinary semiconductor index holder had. The 3× Nasdaq fund returned **45.5% a year** for ten years. These are not near-misses; they are life-changing outcomes, and anyone who tells you leveraged funds are simply a trap has not looked at them.

And in the same decade, with the same structure, the 3× small-cap fund left its holders with **less than the plain index** — and over five years it lost money outright, **−4.4% a year while small caps gained 6.9%**. The 3× Korea fund did the same.

Same product design. Same ten years. Outcomes from seven times the index down to below it.

So what separated them? Two forces, and neither is luck.

## The first: a machine that buys high and sells low, every day

The promise is three times the move of the index **for a single day**. Not three times your year.

Here is why one day does not add up to one year.

You put in $100. To give you 3× exposure, the fund holds $300 of the market.

**Day one, the market rises 10%.** That $300 becomes $330, so you have gained $30. Your money is now $130 — and to keep you at 3×, the fund must hold $390. It buys another $60 of the market. *After it has gone up.*

**Day two, the market falls 10%.** But now the fall applies to $390, not $330. You lose $39. You are left with $91.

Meanwhile the index went up 10% and down 10%, which leaves it at 99 — down 1%.

Three times a 1% loss should be 3%. You lost **9%**. No fee was charged and nobody made a mistake. The fund simply had to buy more at the top of day one to keep its promise, and that larger position met the fall on day two.

That is the machine. Every day the market rises, it buys more. Every day the market falls, it sells. It is required to, because the promise is defined against whatever the fund is worth *today*.

**Which explains both halves of the chart above.** In a market that climbs steadily, buying more after every up day is exactly the right move, and the machine hands you more than three times the index — that is the semiconductor and Nasdaq decade. In a market that lurches up and down without going anywhere, the same machine buys high and sells low, over and over, by design. That is small caps, and that is Korea.

Our own model puts numbers on the boundary. An index compounding at 25% a year with only 15% volatility hands a 3× holder about **83%** a year — more than the 75% that "three times" implies. But at a 10% index return, the 3× fund beats the naive multiple only while volatility stays under about **9%**. Above that, the chop starts taking more than the leverage adds.

The Korea episode shows the losing side happening in real time. The 3× fund peaked on **1 June 2026**. The index it tracks kept climbing, and peaked on **18 June** — seventeen days later. The market went on making new highs the leveraged fund never saw again.

<LeveragePeakChart />

Those seventeen days are the whole argument in miniature, so they are worth slowing down on.

The index finished the stretch **1.2% higher**. To get there it moved an average of **4.6% every single day** — one session up 11.5%, another down 14.1%, nine down days against four up days. Add up all those daily moves regardless of direction and the market travelled **59%** in order to end up 1.2% ahead.

The fund had to respond to every one of those moves. Up 11.5%? Buy more, at the top. Down 14.1%? Sell, at the bottom. Thirteen times in a row, mechanically, because its promise is defined against whatever it is worth that morning.

The index kept 1.2%. The fund lost **13.8%**.

That is the clearest statement of what these products cost: not a fee, not a fall in the market, but the price of being made to trade in and out of a market that could not make up its mind.

## The second: a borrowing bill you cannot see

The fund's published fee is 1.32% a year. That number is true and close to irrelevant.

To hold $300 of market for your $100, it has to get the other $200 somewhere. It rents it, through contracts with investment banks, and pays them a short-term interest rate plus a spread — around 4.5% a year in mid-2026, on the borrowed portion.

None of that is in the 1.32%. The fund's own fee agreement excludes "swap financing and related costs", then adds, in its own words: **"If these expenses were included, the expense ratio would be higher."**

How much higher is public record. Its regulatory filing for 30 April 2026 lists swaps with a face value of **US$3.86 billion** against **US$1.55 billion** of fund assets, at rates between 4.34% and 5.34%. That is roughly **US$176 million a year — about 11% of everything in the fund.**

Some of that comes back. The same filing shows about half the fund's assets held directly in the ordinary index fund, which implies the other half sits in cash as collateral. If that cash earns something close to the same short-term rate, it returns roughly 1.8%, bringing the financing bill down to about **9.5%**.

Add the published fee and the real cost of holding is near **11% a year**, of which the headline 1.32% is roughly an eighth. Not a scandal. That is simply what borrowing costs. But it means the fund starts each year eleven points behind.

The number to watch is the last one. At 30% volatility — roughly a single-country market — the index must compound at about **13.5% a year, or 3.2% a quarter,** before a 3× holder makes anything at all. Below that, you lose money in a rising market.

## "But these are for short-term trading"

That defence is right, and much stricter than most people realise. The fund's own horizon is not "short term". It is **one day**.

Three months is not a rounding error against that. At 30% volatility, the daily rebalancing costs around 6.5% over a quarter and the borrowing adds roughly 2.6% more — so about **9% disappears in three months before the index does anything.**

The Korea episode settles it. The 3× fund fell **76%** from its June peak while the index fell **28%**, and that took about eight weeks. Anyone holding "just a quarter" held through the whole thing.

One point in the machine's favour, since it is owed. On the way down it works in reverse: as the fund shrinks it sells, so it carries less exposure into each subsequent fall. Three times a 28% drop would have been 85%, and the fund lost 76%. The daily reset is not simply a penalty — it is a **volatility cost, not a direction cost.** What it punishes is chop.

## Singapore has its own version, and it goes to seven times

None of this is a distant American problem.

The Singapore Exchange has listed **Daily Leverage Certificates** since 2017 — the first market in Asia to do so. They offer fixed daily leverage of **2, 3, 5 and 7 times**, long or short, on indices and on single stocks. The single-stock list includes American names like Apple, Nvidia and Tesla, Hong Kong names like Tencent and Alibaba, and Singapore's own: DBS, OCBC, UOB, Singtel, Singapore Airlines.

They work the same way, and the exchange says so plainly:

> "The performance of the underlying asset and the DLC is reset at the end of each trading day… Over the period of more than one day, the profits or losses are thus compounded."

To its credit, SGX publishes worked examples that include the uncomfortable case — a three-day sequence where the underlying finishes higher and the certificate finishes down. The information is there for anyone who looks.

These products carry an extra feature ordinary leveraged funds do not: an **air bag**, which suspends trading when the underlying moves sharply against the certificate — 10% for a 5× or 7× index product, 15% for a 5× single stock. It limits the damage from one violent session. It also, in the issuer's own words, leaves the certificate with "a reduced exposure… thereby reducing its ability to recover losses" if the market turns back in your favour afterwards.

**What stands between an ordinary investor and 7× leverage on a single bank stock is one form.** These are classified as Specified Investment Products, so a broker must run a Customer Account Review first. You pass by holding a relevant qualification, or three years of relevant work experience, or six such trades in the past three years — or by passing a free online quiz.

Worth knowing: on 15 May 2026 the Monetary Authority concluded a consultation that would **remove the mandatory financial-advice requirement** for complex products, replacing it with alerts and learning modules. The legislation has not been drafted yet, so today's rules still stand. But the direction is toward fewer gates, not more, which puts rather more weight on the arithmetic above.

## So can you hold one long term?

The decade says: sometimes, spectacularly — and you cannot know which in advance, because the thing that decides it is not whether you picked the right market. It is whether that market happened to trend or churn while you held it.

That distinction is worth being precise about, because the risk people prepare for is usually the other one. If you borrow to invest, the danger is that the market falls and the debt does not — painful, and widely understood. That loan does not care what route the price took: down 20% then up 25% leaves you where you started. A daily-reset fund does care. The rebalancing happens whether you like it or not, the borrowing bill runs whatever the market does, and every swing takes a bite on the way through.

The people holding the 3× semiconductor fund through the last decade were not smarter than the people holding 3× small caps. They were in a market that went one way.

So the question is not whether you are right about the market. It is whether the thing you are holding can survive being right slowly.

#### Where our numbers are soft

The 1.8% credited back for interest on collateral is **our estimate, not a disclosed figure** — the filing shows the cash position but not the income earned on it. Treat the 11% all-in cost as an approximation with its working shown rather than a reported number. It also leaves out a third, smaller drag: the trading costs the fund incurs turning over its own book each day, which no filing separates out.

Our figures also come from one fund over particular windows, which is a fair thing to be suspicious of. The underlying relationship is neither ours nor new: Avellaneda and Zhang derived it formally and tested it across 56 leveraged funds, and independent work has checked it against more than a century of data across eight equity markets. More recent research argues the picture is subtler still — that outcomes turn on whether a market trends or mean-reverts, not on volatility alone, which is consistent with the two-sided result above.
